Background & trajectory

I started out in mechanical engineering, thinking it was the best way to combine the physics and math I'd always enjoyed. I soon realized that what drew me in most was the physics underneath the engineering — understanding why things work at a fundamental level. So I moved toward it directly. I earned a place in Berkeley's physics program, one of the most demanding anywhere, and came out of it thinking about problems more rigorously: from the ground up, without shortcuts. That approach still shapes how I work.

I chose Materials Science and Engineering for my master's because it combines fundamental physics with the engineering of real systems. I'm now a graduate researcher at Duke working on low-dimensional quantum materials across theory, computation, and experiment. In the lab, I fabricate van der Waals heterostructures — exfoliating atomically thin crystals, stacking them layer by layer, and characterizing them with atomic force microscopy and Raman spectroscopy. Computationally, I use first-principles density functional theory to model these systems and extract quantities like proximity-induced spin–orbit coupling at engineered interfaces. I also work on the underlying theory: the band structure, topology, and symmetry that determine how these materials behave.

Much of my work connects to semiconductor physics — how low-dimensional and quantum materials can extend conventional semiconductor technology, including quantum computing platforms that some of my fabrication work supports. I'm interested in the point where these materials move from objects of study to things you can build with, and I'd like to help close that gap. To me, research and building aren't separate: understanding something from first principles is what makes it possible to build something durable.
