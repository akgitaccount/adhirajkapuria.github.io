Research / Theory

Graphene, band topology, and proximity effects

My theoretical interest is in how the Dirac physics of graphene is modified by stacking, symmetry, and interfaces, giving rise to unconventional quantum Hall states, topological flat bands, and proximity-induced superconductivity.

Dirac fermions and the quantum Hall effect

Low-energy electrons in monolayer graphene behave as massless Dirac fermions, described near the K and K′ valleys by

$$H = \hbar v_F\,(\xi\,\sigma_x k_x + \sigma_y k_y),$$

with linear dispersion $E = \pm\hbar v_F|k|$ and a Berry phase of $\pi$. This Berry phase has a direct experimental consequence. In a magnetic field, the Landau spectrum contains a level fixed at zero energy, and the Hall conductivity is quantized at half-integer values, $\sigma_{xy} = \pm 4\,(N+\tfrac{1}{2})\,e^2/h$. Bilayer graphene, whose low-energy electrons are massive chiral fermions with Berry phase $2\pi$, instead shows a double-height step in $\sigma_{xy}$ across zero density. The quantum Hall response therefore reflects the chirality and topology of the underlying bands.

Rhombohedral multilayers, flat bands, and Chern states

Stacking order sets the low-energy dispersion. In rhombohedral (ABC) $N$-layer graphene, the two-band model gives a chiral dispersion $E \sim k^N$ that flattens with layer number. These flat bands enhance interaction effects, favoring correlated and topological states: with a displacement field or interaction-driven symmetry breaking, the gapped bands can carry nonzero Chern number. In rhombohedral pentalayer graphene, this produces correlated and Chern-insulating states, with observed Chern numbers $C = -5$ and $C = -3$.

The origin of the $C = -3$ state, and the transition from $C = -3$ to $C = -5$, can be explained by an asynchronous mass inversion mechanism — the subject of a companion presentation I prepared. Trigonal warping splits the low-energy bands near each valley into a central touching point and three satellite Dirac cones, and the combination of staggered layer order and displacement field gives them different, momentum-dependent masses. As the displacement field increases, the satellite cones invert first — changing the Chern number by $\pm 3$ to give $C = -3$ — and the central point inverts at a higher field, completing the transition to $C = -5$. Without trigonal warping the system would pass directly from $C = 0$ to $C = -5$, so the satellite cones are what generate the intermediate state.

Proximity coupling to WSe₂

Graphene's intrinsic spin–orbit coupling is negligible, but coupling it to a transition-metal dichalcogenide such as WSe₂ induces a stronger effective coupling by proximity, dominated by an Ising (valley–Zeeman) term that locks spin to valley. This has measurable consequences for superconductivity: in bilayer graphene proximitized by WSe₂, superconductivity appears at zero magnetic field, with a critical temperature roughly an order of magnitude higher and over a wider density range than in bilayer graphene alone. Superconductivity emerges only for displacement fields that push the electronic wavefunctions toward the WSe₂, indicating that the induced Ising spin–orbit coupling stabilizes the pairing, consistent with the observed violation of the Pauli limit under in-plane fields. This is the regime that motivates my interest in graphene/WSe₂ interfaces and in quantifying induced spin–orbit parameters from first principles.
